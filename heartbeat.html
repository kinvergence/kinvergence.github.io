The Heartbeat

One hour a week, and everyone speaks.

This is the engine. It is the only part we would ask a family to start with, and it is the only part we have watched work for four years.

It needs two or three relatives who are each building something of their own — a business, a practice, a body of creative work, anything self-directed. It costs an hour.

We call it the Heartbeat because that is what it does: a regular pulse whose absence would be noticed. Same slot every week. Video, so faces are visible. Everyone speaks, in turn, every time.

The agenda

Ours took about a year to settle into this, and it still moves. Treat it as a starting shape, not a specification.

  1. Who you are and what you are working on, in a sentence. As you would say it to a stranger. It sounds like a warm-up and it is not. It is where you hear your own description of your work out loud and notice that it stopped being true months ago. In our family this began as elevator-pitch practice and it has never left the top of the agenda.
  2. What you got done. What you are doing next. What you learned — including from what failed. Keep it short. The value is not in the reporting; it is that everyone else now knows where you are, and can offer something in the last item.
  3. What you changed about how you work. This is the first thing groups drop and the one most worth keeping. It is how an improvement one person found reaches everybody else — the tool, the habit, the way of scheduling a week. In our family this item is older than the meeting's name.
  4. What the next in-person gathering is for. Two minutes a week, and a month of four people working separately quietly acquires a shared shape.
  5. Open discussion. No agenda at all. This is where the actual helping gets arranged, which is exactly why it is not optional and why it should not be the item that gets cut when the meeting runs long.

Three things that are easy to get wrong

These are the failure modes we either hit ourselves or watched ourselves nearly hit.

  • Weekly, not monthly. Families fall out of alignment continuously, just from living — different weeks, different pressures, different information. If the meetings come round more slowly than that drift, each one spends its time re-establishing context and never gets to anything else. If an hour is too expensive, shorten the meeting. Do not space it out.
  • Go when you have nothing to report. This is the counterintuitive one and it is close to the whole practice. The week somebody has nothing to show is exactly the week the rest of the family needs to hear from that person — and exactly the week the temptation to skip is strongest. A meeting that only happens when there is news is not a rhythm; it is a status update, and it will stop.
  • Expect it to feel thin, and expect to get the details wrong. The first three months are supposed to feel thin. That is where most groups quit, before the thing has had enough repetitions to do anything at all. Our agenda took about a year of adjusting and the tools underneath it took considerably longer. Start with something that repeats and fix it as you go.

What to skip

Skip the name, the logo, the website, the legal entity, and the shared tools. Every one of them feels like starting. Every one of them is a reliable way of not starting.

We are the evidence for that. We spent twenty months with a genuine agreement, real goodwill, and an open question about what tooling to adopt — and almost nothing repeated until somebody wrote down an agenda and put a recurring appointment on the calendar. We set up nothing purpose-built until after the practice was already working, using a group chat we happened to have. Our tools followed the rhythm by nearly two years. They have never once led it.

What comes after

Three more things we do. None of them is where a family should start, and all three arrived years after the weekly call did.

  • A monthly gathering, in person. We call it Demo Day. Everybody shows the work rather than describing it, which is a different and much harder thing. Two requirements carry it and both are the first to be relaxed by a group under pressure: every member brings something showable, and people are in the same room. We started ours two years in.
  • A standing agreement to be available in between. Accountability, sanity checks, big-picture reviews, encouragement. We named these on the first day in 2020 and then did not write them down again for four and a half years — which tells you something about how much a practice with no scheduled occasion leaves behind.
  • An agreement the family writes with itself. A charter. Write it late. Ours came four and a half years in and was written backwards, from what we had actually been doing. A charter written at the start is a guess; a charter written late is a record.

There is also a yearly layer we did not plan and are still working out: each year we pick one thing to strengthen about how we operate. It has never been a meeting. It seems to be the family deciding what to work on about itself.

Put it on the calendar.

That is the whole ask. Pick a slot, invite two or three relatives, use the agenda above, and hold it for three months before judging it.

If you do, we would genuinely rather hear what did not work than hear that you liked the idea. Tell the person who sent you here.

What we are still unsure about

Kinvergence is a joint creation of Scherer-Frailey Ventures. The practices are years of four people's work and the name was chosen together. Who holds it has not been settled, and no license has been granted yet. We would rather say so than imply otherwise.

The practices themselves are methods, and methods are not the kind of thing anyone owns. Nothing stops a family from running them today.
