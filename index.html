Four relatives agreed to help each other build things. It took almost two years to find a shape that stuck.

They talked the whole time — informally, mostly one to one, still working largely alone. What finally held was smaller than any of that: an agenda, and a repeating slot on the calendar. They have met almost every week since 2022. This is what they do, and we would like to know whether it works for any family but ours.

One

What one family actually did

Scherer-Frailey Ventures. Four self-employed adults in one extended family — two siblings, and the two sons of one of them — living in four separate households and working in four fields with nothing to do with each other: software and tooling, music teaching, creative media and visual art, and software consulting.

In September 2020 they agreed to help each other more deliberately. The message that started it was modest to the point of being vague, and deliberately so:

“The exact form of collaboration is not important — what's important is forming an agreement to be available, so that the solo entrepreneur is less constrained and more enabled to plan.”

The founding message, 11 September 2020

Everyone was in. Everyone meant it. And for nearly two years it stayed exactly that informal.

People talked — often, and mostly one to one. They swapped links, read each other's drafts, and encouraged each other through some genuinely hard stretches. But each of them was still pushing a venture forward largely alone, and there was no occasion that obliged anybody to show up. Very little of that period left a record. The group's own reading of that now, having thought about it a great deal, is not that nothing was happening: it is that a practice which obliges no artifact leaves no trace, which turns out to be one of the more useful things this family has worked out about itself.

What changed was small. In May 2022 one member proposed a three-item agenda and a standing weekly slot — go around the room with a short status, then open discussion, then talk about the tools and habits you use on your work rather than in it. Two weeks later the meeting had a name. A month after that, somebody suggested they all practice introducing themselves, and that became the first item. The same conversation proposed getting together in person on the first of August to demonstrate what each of them was working on rather than describe it.

None of it was designed. It accumulated. The agenda has been revised, the meeting has been shortened and lengthened, and the tools underneath it have been chosen, configured, argued about and replaced more than once. What has not changed is that it repeats: the call has happened almost every week since, and the in-person gathering roughly every month — through stretches when members had a great deal to show and stretches when they had nothing at all.

  1. Sept 2020

    An agreement, and no schedule. Four relatives agree to be available to each other. Nothing is put on a calendar.

  2. 2020–2022

    Informal. Real conversations, mostly one to one, largely unrecorded. Everyone still building alone.

  3. May 2022

    An agenda and a standing slot. The first thing that repeats.

  4. June 2022

    It gets a name. The weekly call becomes the Heartbeat.

  5. Aug 2022

    The first Demo Day. Everyone shows something instead of describing it.

  6. Since

    Almost every week, refined continuously. In person roughly monthly. Still going.

The two years are the part worth taking away. Four willing people, a genuinely good idea, and a standing offer of help produced a great deal of goodwill and not much that repeated. Nobody became more committed in May 2022 — somebody wrote down an agenda and put a recurring appointment on the calendar, and that turned out to be the whole difference.

Everything after it has been refinement, and the refinement is still going.

Nobody set out to build a framework. In 2026 the four of them separated the pattern from the family that found it, gave it a name, and wrote it down so that another family could try it. That writing is all public, and this is the entire evidence base behind it: one family.

That matters, and there is more on it further down.

Two

What we want this to become

The people best positioned to help each other are very often already related — and nothing brings them together on purpose. Kinvergence is a way of doing it on purpose, kept as small as it can be and still work.

We are not trying to grow it, and we are not trying to be the ones who help. If a family runs this and never tells us, that is a success. If a family reads every word here, ignores all of it, and simply starts calling each other every week, that is also a success. The practices are methods, and methods are not the kind of thing anyone owns.

What we are actually short of is not interest. It is a second family.

What it will not become

  • Not a course or a program. There is nothing to complete. Anything that can be finished is not this.
  • Not a consultancy. The moment running someone else's group is a service we sell, it stops being something a family does for itself.
  • Not a movement with a leader. One of us wrote most of it down. That is authorship, not authority.
  • Not an app or a platform. A tool would quietly replace the practice, and the practice is the entire thing.
  • Not a vocabulary. Somebody proposed inventing terms for all of it. We declined. The objection that carried: it is a meeting, and a meeting already has a name.
  • Not a thing that outgrows its own advice. We tell an adopting family to skip the name, the logo, and the website, because all three feel like starting and are a reliable way of not starting. We have already broken that rule once. Everything downstream of it stays deliberately small — including this page.

The longer version was written as a proposal to the four of us rather than as an announcement, and it has not been agreed yet: read the vision.

Three

Enough to try one thing

Of the four things this family does, one is the engine, and it is the one a family can start next week. It costs an hour. It needs two or three relatives who are each building something of their own.

It is called the Heartbeat, because that is what it does — a regular pulse whose absence would be noticed. Same slot every week. Video. Everyone speaks, in turn, every time.

  1. Who you are and what you are working on, in a sentence. As you would say it to a stranger. It sounds like a warm-up. It is not: it is where a member hears the familiar description of the work out loud and notices it stopped being true months ago.
  2. What you got done. What you are doing next. What you learned — including from what failed. What you changed about how you work. That last one is the first thing groups drop and the one most worth keeping. It is how an improvement one member found reaches everybody else.
  3. What the next in-person gathering is for. Two minutes, once a week, and a month of four people working separately acquires a shared shape.
  4. Open discussion. No agenda at all. This is where the actual helping gets arranged, which is why it is not optional.

Three things that are easy to get wrong

  • Weekly, not monthly. Families fall out of alignment continuously, just from living. If the meetings come round slower than that, they stop adding up. If an hour is too expensive, shorten the meeting — do not space it out.
  • Go when you have nothing to report. This is the counterintuitive one, and it is close to the whole practice. The week a member has nothing to show is exactly the week the others need to hear from that member — and exactly the week the temptation to skip is strongest.
  • Expect the first three months to feel thin. They are supposed to. That is where most groups quit — before the thing has had enough repetitions to do anything at all.
  • Expect to get the details wrong for a while. The agenda above took the first family about a year of adjusting, and the tools underneath it took considerably longer. Start with something that repeats and fix it as you go.

Skip, for now: the name, the logo, the website, the legal entity, and the shared tools. Every one of them feels like starting. Every one of them is a way of not starting.

The family above is the evidence. They spent two years discovering that goodwill on its own is no substitute for a recurring appointment — and they set nothing purpose-built up until after the practice was already working, using a group chat they happened to have. The tools followed the rhythm. They have never once led it.

There is more, and it can wait. A monthly gathering where everybody shows the work rather than describing it. A standing agreement to be available in between. An agreement the family writes with itself — years in, once there is something true to write down.

Four

An honest ask

The best thing you could do with this page is close it and put a call on the calendar.

You do not need us for any of it. There is nothing to join, nothing to sign, no community waiting on the other side, and nobody keeping track. We would rather say that plainly than put a button here that nobody answers.

But there is one thing we want, and it is worth being direct about.

We are looking for one more family. Not to approve of this — to try it for a few months and then tell us what we got wrong. That is worth considerably more to us than being agreed with, because everything written here generalizes from a single family, and we cannot tell from the inside how much of it was the framework and how much was just us. A second family would find that out almost immediately. It is the single strongest reason this page exists.

If any of that sounds like your family: tell the person who sent you here that you would like to talk. That is not a placeholder for a form — it is genuinely how this works, and it is exactly how the four of them started. One conversation, with somebody you already know.

What else we do not know

Carried in the open, because a framework that hides these is a pitch.

  • Weekly works. We do not know what else would. Nobody has tested a gentler rhythm, and nobody has measured how fast a family actually drifts.
  • The monthly gathering is claimed to need to be in person. That claim has never been tested against a family that could not gather.
  • The least visible practice is the least evidenced. Years of being available to each other left almost no written record, so much of what we have written about it is reasoning rather than observation. We label it as such.
  • It assumes goodwill. The framework says nothing about family conflict and cannot. Where the willingness to help is not already there, no rhythm will supply it.
  • Nobody has ever asked a family outside this one whether they want any of this. As far as we know, you are among the first to be asked.

Kinvergence is a joint creation of Scherer-Frailey Ventures. The practices are years of four people's work and the name was chosen together. Who holds it has not been settled, and no license has been granted yet. We would rather say so than imply otherwise.

The practices themselves are methods, and methods are not the kind of thing anyone owns. Nothing stops a family from running them today.
